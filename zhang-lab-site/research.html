Research

We study how microbial communities shape host physiology through conserved signaling pathways, with an emphasis on aging, reproduction, gut function, and environmental stress responses.

1. Microbiome regulation of life-history traits

We use defined microbial communities and natural bacterial isolates to determine how microbes influence development, reproduction, stress resistance, and lifespan in C. elegans. Our work asks which bacterial traits drive beneficial or detrimental host outcomes, and whether these effects reflect predictable tradeoffs.

2. Insulin/IGF-1 signaling in host–microbe interactions

We investigate how hosts sense and respond to microbial inputs through insulin-like peptides and conserved endocrine signaling pathways. By combining genetics, reporter strains, and imaging, we examine how microbial composition reshapes signaling across tissues including the intestine and nervous system.

3. Dysbiosis and aging

Age-associated changes in microbial composition can alter gut colonization, intestinal integrity, and late-life physiology. We study how host genotype and microbiome structure interact during aging, and how dysbiosis contributes to host decline.

4. Environmental stressors and biological resilience

In collaboration with environmental health researchers, we examine how environmentally persistent free radicals and related pollutants affect host survival, mitochondrial stress responses, gut function, and microbial ecology.

5. Quantitative and predictive biology

We are developing high-throughput imaging workflows, computational pipelines, and machine learning approaches to connect microbial features with host phenotypes and to identify bacterial combinations that promote healthy aging.
